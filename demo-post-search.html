Criminal defense

Can an officer search your car at a traffic stop?

Consent, plain view and probable cause decide most roadside searches. Knowing the difference keeps a stop from becoming a case.

September 21, 2026Criminal defenseAbout 4 min

Three ways a car gets searched

Roadside searches in Georgia almost always rest on one of three things: you agreed, something was in plain view, or the officer had probable cause. Knowing which one is in play tells you a lot about how the case will go.

Consent

"Mind if I take a look?" is a question, not an instruction. People say yes because saying no feels like an admission, and because the question is phrased to sound like a formality.

You can decline. Declining is not evidence of anything and it is not obstruction. "I do not consent to a search" is the whole sentence. If the officer searches anyway on some other basis, that basis is now something your attorney can examine, which is exactly what you want.

Consent is the only one of the three that you hand over yourself. It is also the hardest to challenge later.

Plain view

If an officer is lawfully standing where they are and can see something obviously incriminating, they do not need permission to act on it. That is why where things sit in a car matters, and why a lawful stop that lasts longer than it should is worth examining.

Probable cause

This is a real standard, not a feeling. The officer has to be able to articulate specific facts. "Nervous behavior" alone has been tested in Georgia courts many times and does not carry the weight officers sometimes think it does. The smell of marijuana has gotten more complicated since hemp became legal, because the two are not distinguishable by smell.

How long a stop can last

A traffic stop is supposed to last as long as the traffic business takes. Writing the citation, checking the license and registration, and that is it. Extending the stop to wait on a dog or to run an unrelated investigation requires its own justification. This is one of the most productive areas for a defense attorney, because the timestamps are usually on video.

What to do in the moment

  • Stay polite. Nothing here requires being difficult, and being difficult never helps.
  • Decline a search clearly, once, out loud.
  • Do not physically resist a search that happens anyway. Argue it later, where arguing works.
  • Note the time the stop started and when anything else arrived. Those two numbers matter.
