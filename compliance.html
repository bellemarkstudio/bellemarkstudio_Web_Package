Compliance and ethics

The rules behind an AI receptionist for a law firm.

A law firm is not a plumbing company. Calls to a firm can carry privileged and sensitive information, and the lawyers who own the phone line answer to bar rules, state statutes and federal telephone law. This page lays out which rules touch an AI receptionist, where each comes from, and what Bellemark does about it.

Responsibility stays with the firm. Under the professional conduct rules, the lawyers remain responsible for how client and prospective-client information is handled, for supervising non-lawyer help and technology, and for what is said on the firm's behalf. We design for that division of responsibility instead of pretending a vendor can take it over.

01 · Unauthorized practice of law

The receptionist takes information. It does not give legal advice.

The line between intake and legal advice is the most important design limit for any non-lawyer, human or automated, who speaks to the public on a firm's behalf.

What the rules say

  • Georgia defines the practice of law and bars unlicensed practice by statute. O.C.G.A. §§ 15-19-50 and 15-19-51
  • Lawyers may not assist someone else in practicing law without a license. Model Rule 5.5(a); Georgia Rule 5.5
  • Florida Bar Advisory Opinion 24-1 says an AI intake chatbot should say it is not a lawyer, keep its questions to factual information, and send legal questions back to the lawyer. Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 (2024)
  • Giving a caller a view on whether they have a case, their odds, deadlines that apply to them, or whether the firm will take them on all lean toward advice or an engagement decision, which belongs to the lawyer.

What Bellemark does

  • Scripts the receptionist for intake only: name, number, short reason for the call, and urgency.
  • Instructs it to decline legal questions, say it cannot give advice, and route the caller to the firm.
  • Never has it promise representation, a callback time the firm has not set, or an outcome.
  • Tests those limits before launch with questions callers actually ask, and shows you the results.

No language model can be guaranteed never to say something it should not. That is why the script is limited, your attorneys approve it, and call logs are available for review (see Supervision).

02 · Confidentiality

What a caller tells the receptionist is information the firm must protect.

Confidentiality is broader than privilege. It covers nearly everything a lawyer learns about a matter, including from someone who never becomes a client.

What the rules say

  • A lawyer must not reveal information relating to the representation without informed consent or another listed exception, and must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to or disclosure of it. Model Rule 1.6(a), (c); Georgia Rule 1.6
  • Duty of technological competence is part of the duty of competence. Model Rule 1.1, comment [8]
  • ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) says a lawyer must evaluate the risk of inputting client information into a generative AI tool, and that "self-learning" tools that may disclose that information to others can require the client's informed consent, with boilerplate in an engagement letter not enough. ABA Formal Op. 512
  • The Florida opinion makes the same point: confirm the provider has to preserve confidentiality, look at its security, and find out whether it retains what is submitted. Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1

What Bellemark does

  • Collects the minimum: contact details and a short reason for the call, with the greeting inviting the caller to keep details for the attorney.
  • Gives the firm, in writing before launch, a list of every vendor that processes call audio or text, where data is stored, who at Bellemark can access it, how long it is kept, and how it is deleted if you leave.
  • Tells you what each vendor's terms say about using data to train models, and turns off training or data sharing wherever the platform offers that setting.
  • Uses firm data only to run that firm's intake. It is never used to market to other firms or sold.

03 · Prospective clients and conflicts

People who call but never sign still have rights.

The firm's first contact with a possible client is usually a phone call, which is exactly where an automated receptionist sits.

What the rules say

  • A person who consults a lawyer about possibly forming a client-lawyer relationship is a prospective client. The lawyer must not use or reveal what was learned, even if no relationship follows. Model Rule 1.18(a)-(b); Georgia Rule 1.18
  • Information from a prospective client can disqualify the lawyer from a later matter if it could be significantly harmful to that person. A firm can limit the exposure by limiting the initial information it takes in. Model Rule 1.18(c)-(d) and comment [4]
  • Conflict checks run on the information collected. Intake data therefore has to be accurate enough to feed the firm's conflict process. Model Rules 1.7, 1.9, 1.10; Georgia counterparts
  • Florida's opinion suggests screening questions so a chatbot limits its conversations with people already represented by another lawyer. Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1

What Bellemark does

  • Keeps the script to what is needed to identify the caller and decide who should follow up. It does not collect case facts, adverse-party details or documents.
  • Captures names of other parties only if your attorneys tell us to, so your conflict process can use them.
  • Can ask whether the caller already has a lawyer on the matter, if your attorneys want that screen.
  • Routes every inquiry to a person at your firm. The receptionist does not accept or decline matters.

04 · AI disclosure

Callers are told they are speaking with an automated assistant.

Pretending to be a person is the fastest way to create an ethics problem, a consumer-protection problem and a trust problem at the same time.

What the rules say

  • Florida Bar Opinion 24-1 says a lawyer must inform prospective clients that they are communicating with an AI program. It also holds the lawyer responsible if a chatbot gives misleading information or communicates in an intrusive or coercive way. Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1
  • ABA Formal Opinion 512 ties disclosure to the duty to communicate with clients and to the rules against false or misleading communications. Model Rules 1.4, 7.1; ABA Formal Op. 512
  • Some state statutes require AI disclosure in particular settings. Utah amended its AI disclosure law in 2025 and addresses regulated occupations, which is a reason to check each state where the firm takes calls. Utah S.B. 226 (2025)
  • There is no single federal or Georgia rule that applies one disclosure sentence to every law firm call. The ethics opinions and state statutes above are why disclosure is the safe default.

What Bellemark does

  • Opens every call with a greeting that says the caller has reached the firm's automated assistant. The wording is yours to approve.
  • Never has the receptionist claim to be a person, paralegal, intake specialist or attorney, and instructs it to answer honestly if asked.
  • Lets callers ask for a live person and routes that request to the firm.

05 · Supervision of technology and vendors

The lawyers supervise the receptionist the way they supervise any non-lawyer help.

What the rules say

  • Partners and supervising lawyers must make reasonable efforts to ensure the firm has measures giving reasonable assurance that non-lawyers' conduct fits the lawyer's obligations. Model Rules 5.1, 5.3; Georgia Rules 5.1, 5.3
  • ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 applies those rules to outsourcing, including vetting the provider and its confidentiality safeguards. ABA Formal Op. 08-451 (2008)
  • ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies Rules 5.1 and 5.3 to generative AI: firms should have policies, training and oversight, and should review output rather than rely on it. ABA Formal Op. 512
  • The State Bar of Georgia publishes AI resources for lawyers, and other states have issued their own opinions (Florida, for one, above). Your counsel should check the current guidance for each state the firm practices in.

What Bellemark does

  • Sets up the system so the firm can review call logs and transcripts, then walks your staff through where to find them.
  • Sends urgent and new-inquiry alerts to named people at your firm, so a person always follows up.
  • Documents the setup in a one-page summary your attorneys can file as part of their supervision records.
  • Treats any change to what the receptionist says as a change that needs your approval.

06 · Privilege and AI tools

Privilege is fragile, and courts are only beginning to address AI.

Attorney-client privilege depends on a confidential communication made for the purpose of getting legal advice. Whether and when an AI tool sits inside that protection is not settled law.

What the law says

  • A federal trial court in New York held in February 2026 that a criminal defendant's conversations with a public generative AI tool were not protected by attorney-client privilege or work product. United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 13, 2026)
  • That is one district court decision on a consumer AI tool. It does not bind a Georgia court and it did not involve a law firm's own intake system, but it is a signal that courts may not treat what is typed or said to an AI tool as automatically protected.
  • A first call to a firm is often before any engagement, so privilege may not attach to everything said on it in any case. The confidentiality duties above still apply.

What Bellemark does

  • Designs intake to avoid substantive legal discussion, which keeps less sensitive content on the system to begin with.
  • Does not use public consumer AI chat products to handle your callers' information.
  • Tells you plainly in the data summary which vendors touch the information, so your attorneys can judge the privilege and confidentiality risk themselves.

07 · Call recording and wiretap law

Recording and transcribing calls is regulated by federal and state law.

An AI receptionist usually records and transcribes. Whether that needs the caller's consent depends on where the caller is, not only where the firm is.

What the law says

  • Federal law allows recording when one party to the call consents. 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d)
  • Georgia follows one-party consent for a person who is a party to the call. O.C.G.A. § 16-11-66
  • Several states require all parties to consent, including California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington. Connecticut imposes civil liability unless all parties consent, and a few states (Delaware, Michigan) are ambiguous. Justia 50-State Survey, Recording Phone Calls and Conversations
  • For calls that cross state lines, the safe practice is to follow the stricter state's rule. The California Supreme Court applied California's all-party rule to a call between a California resident and someone in a one-party state. Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney, Inc. (Cal. 2006)
  • California's Invasion of Privacy Act has been used against AI voice vendors as well as the businesses using them. In February 2025 a federal court in the Northern District of California let a class action against Google's Contact Center AI proceed past a motion to dismiss, adopting a "capability" test under which a vendor that could use call data for its own benefit can be treated as a third-party eavesdropper. That is a ruling at the pleading stage, not a final judgment. Ambriz v. Google LLC (N.D. Cal. Feb. 10, 2025); Cal. Penal Code §§ 631, 632, 632.7

What Bellemark does

  • Builds a recording and AI notice into the opening greeting so every caller hears it before anything is collected, regardless of state.
  • Treats the all-party standard as the default so a caller in California, Florida or Illinois is covered the same way as a caller in Georgia.
  • Tells you in writing which vendors process the audio and what their terms say about using call data for their own purposes, since that is the question the Ambriz line of cases turns on.
  • Lets your counsel adjust the notice wording for the states your firm covers.

08 · Telephone and text law

Answering calls is one thing. Calling and texting people back is another.

What the law says

  • The Telephone Consumer Protection Act and the FCC's rules restrict certain autodialed and artificial or prerecorded voice calls and texts to consumers. 47 U.S.C. § 227; 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200
  • In February 2024 the FCC ruled that voices generated with AI are "artificial" voices under the TCPA, so calls using them generally need the called party's prior express consent. FCC Declaratory Ruling, FCC 24-17, CG Docket No. 23-362 (Feb. 2024)
  • That ruling is about calls a business places. A caller who dials your firm and speaks with an answering assistant is a different situation from the firm placing an outbound AI call.
  • Texting is treated as calling under TCPA rules, so any automated text back to a caller, such as a missed-call text, should be set up with counsel's guidance on consent and opt-out handling.
  • Lawyer rules on live telephone solicitation of potential clients apply to the lawyers and anyone acting for them. Model Rule 7.3; Georgia Rule 7.3

What Bellemark does

  • Sets up the receptionist for inbound calls. We do not set up outbound AI voice calls or cold text campaigns for law firms.
  • Where your firm wants an automated follow-up text to a person who just called, we use plain informational wording and include opt-out language, and we ask your attorneys to approve it first.
  • Honors opt-outs and do-not-contact requests by stopping further messages to that number.

10 · Advertising, referrals and fee rules

How Bellemark charges matters to a law firm.

What the rules say

  • Lawyers' communications about their services must not be false or misleading, and the lawyer is responsible for what an automated system says on the firm's behalf. Model Rule 7.1; Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1
  • A lawyer may not give anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer's services, except for reasonable advertising costs. Model Rule 7.2(b); Georgia Rule 7.2
  • A lawyer may not share legal fees with a non-lawyer, with limited exceptions. Model Rule 5.4(a); Georgia Rule 5.4
  • Georgia's advertising and solicitation rules have their own labeling and content requirements that differ from the Model Rules. Check Georgia Rules 7.1 to 7.5 for any text the receptionist or follow-ups use.

What Bellemark does

  • Charges fixed fees for the system: a one-time setup fee and a flat monthly fee. We do not charge per client signed, and we do not take a share of your legal fees.
  • Does not sell or route cases, and does not pay anyone for referring clients to your firm.
  • Keeps all statements about your firm in the receptionist's script to ones you approve.

11 · Data security, breach and health information

Reasonable security, plus a plan for when something goes wrong.

What the rules say

  • Reasonable measures to secure electronic communications with clients, with the level of protection matched to sensitivity. Model Rule 1.6(c); ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)
  • A lawyer's duties after an electronic data breach include notifying affected current clients. ABA Formal Op. 483 (2018)
  • Every state has a breach-notification statute. Georgia's is at O.C.G.A. § 10-1-910 et seq. and its coverage is narrower than some states'. Your counsel should check how it, and the laws of your callers' states, apply.
  • HIPAA generally does not cover a law firm collecting information from its own prospective clients, but it can apply when a firm handles protected health information as a business associate of a covered entity. Many personal injury and medical-related inquiries still involve sensitive health details, so treat that information carefully either way.

What Bellemark does

  • Collects health details only if your attorneys ask for them. The standard script does not.
  • Limits access to your account to the people who need it, and keeps credentials out of shared documents.
  • Agrees in writing to notify you promptly if we learn of unauthorized access to your firm's data, so you can meet your own obligations.
  • Returns or deletes your data when the engagement ends, as set out in the data summary.

13 · Before you go live

A checklist for your attorneys.

  • Read the current rules and ethics opinions in every state the firm practices in or takes calls from, starting with the sources below.
  • Approve the greeting, the questions asked and the limits on what the receptionist will say.
  • Approve the AI and call-recording notice, and decide whether to treat all callers as all-party-consent callers.
  • Read the data handling summary and decide whether the vendors and retention terms meet the firm's confidentiality duties.
  • Decide who receives urgent alerts, who follows up on each inquiry, and how quickly.
  • Set a schedule for reviewing call logs and transcripts.
  • Decide whether any automated text follow-up is used and, if so, who approves its wording.
  • Confirm the fee arrangement fits the firm's referral and fee-sharing rules.
  • Keep a copy of the approved script and setup summary with the firm's supervision records.
